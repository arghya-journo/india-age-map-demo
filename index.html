INDIA'S AGE DIVIDE

India is ageing, but not at one speed. In some districts, seniors already outnumber children. In others, children outnumber seniors five to one. These maps show how sharply the country's age structure changes from district to district.

NFHS-6 (2023–24), 715 district records. Manipur was not covered in NFHS-6. Tap any district on the maps to see its numbers.

Map 1

Where seniors already outnumber children

The traditional population pyramid has already flipped in 32 districts.

Pathanamthitta in Kerala is the clearest example: 28.4% of its population is aged 60 or more, against 15.8% below 15 — about 180 seniors for every 100 children.

The pattern is concentrated in the south, but not confined to it. Sindhudurg and Ratnagiri in Maharashtra, Kinnaur in Himachal Pradesh and Kolkata are among the districts already on the older side of the line.

But another India is still very young

Nuh has the country's highest child share in the NFHS-6 district data: 42.1% of its population is below 15.

Several Meghalaya districts are close behind. In East Jaintia Hills, 40.8% of people are below 15 and only 4.9% are 60 or older — roughly 12 seniors for every 100 children.

Bihar also stands out: Supaul, Khagaria and Araria each have about 40% of their population below 15.

Twenty districts have newly tipped older

These places had more children than seniors in NFHS-5. They no longer do.

Wardha now has about 132 seniors for every 100 children. Kolkata is at about 117; Chennai at 107. Coimbatore, Udupi, Hassan and Kinnaur are also among the new additions.

This is a useful way to read demographic change: not merely as a higher senior share, but as the moment when the balance between the youngest and oldest parts of the population changes sides.

In 97 districts, the shift is happening at both ends

The senior share rose by at least 2 percentage points while the child share fell by at least 2 points.

This group stretches well beyond the oldest districts. It includes parts of West Bengal, Jammu and Kashmir, Assam, Tripura and several southern states.

That does not mean every district is “old”. It means the age structure is moving in the same direction from both ends — fewer children as a share of the population, more seniors.

About the data and the grey areas

NFHS is a survey rather than a census, so district figures are estimates. Earlier NFHS-5 values are used only where the district can be compared safely. The maps use a district boundary layer prepared for NFHS visualisation and then match each shape to the NFHS-6 data using state and district together.

We also use conservative spelling and rename matching within the same state. A district is never matched across state lines just because the name looks similar. Manipur remains hatched because NFHS-6 did not cover it. Any boundary we still cannot match safely is shown in grey rather than filled with an invented value.
